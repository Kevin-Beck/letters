                                                  May 3, 1943
Dear Mother,
     I have a few spare minutes so will attempt to enlighten you somewhat on
the routine life of a sailor. As I have often stated before my letter writing
is full of interruptions. For example, so far I have stoped for a few minutes
after each each of the first two sentences. Now the Captain just finished
telling some plans for to-morrow - thus, from the above you can gather how
difficult a task it is to compose a pleasant, coherent letter not to mention
the limited supply of news. Another important factor is the writer. When you
take everything into consideration you can't expect much. Now that that
subject is settled and completely understood by all I can proceed further
into my explonations.

     In a previous letter I stated I
[line cut off at bottom of scan - probably "would write to you every ..."]

[--- 430503pg2.jpg (marked "-2-") ---]

day and that still holds good. The only catch is my attempts are futile.
(One minute please, we are having Mail Call. Well, I finally received a letter
from home. It was posted in Petrolia and the date isn't clear, but I think it
was April 7. It was the first one from you since [crossed out] April 15. I will
wait awhile to answer it.) I have an excuse for not keeping my promise and no
matter how lame it is it is an excuse. There is always work to be done during
the day and usually some at night. The men haven't been receiving mail either
and are writing plenty of letters tell every one about it. The more they
write, the more I have to censor and lately that has been a big undertaking.
I know how the men feel about having their mail go out so I do my best to get
it censored

[--- 430503pg3.jpg (marked "-3-") ---]

and sent ashore as quickly as possible. That is the picture, the time I spend
censoring mail is the only time I have to write my own letters.

     The work aboard ship is going on as usual but now something new has been
added - night school and guess what they are teaching - Navigation. I am so
thankful that I took that six weeks at Pitt, three months at John Jay, plus
about two months of practical work. It helps me so much in this elementary
course I am now taking. They haven't even changed the book. That just about
covers my undertakings.

     While I was in Galveston I sent a money order home and, as yet, haven't
heard whether or not it was received. I still have the receipts and would
like to do away with them if you have the

[--- 430503pg4.jpg (marked "-4-") ---]

money. Also, I sent a cablegram from one of the ports but I am not sure just
when. Perhaps it was after the 7th so I may hear about it in a latter letter.

     I am quite disappointed that I can't do any thing about Mother's Day. The
choice of gifts is very limited here, if and when I could get ashore to buy
any. The only thing I can do is hope that I will be able to make up for it
next year or perhaps the following year. I will at least be thinking of you
all the more on that day.

     Yesterday I received a V-mail letter from Aunt Lyle dated April 2. Adding
2 and 2 and subtracting the time for the pony express to get to K.C. I have
concluded that Air Mail is the

[--- 430503pg5.jpg (marked "-5-") ---]

faster of the two. Nevertheless, both are welcomed with open arms, hungry
eyes, and then with a happy heart.

     It is now 2245 which is late for me now and I still have some work to do.
I have to make up a routine for to-morrow and there is one part that never
changes, at least never get later - [crossed out] 0600 - Reveille - all hands.
I still don't like to get up early.

     Tell the folks I said hello and that one of these days I will find time
to answer a few letters. I will still try to keep my promise to write to you
every day. I think by now you are accustomed to a few pages of just writing.
Perhaps I should be an artist and illustrate some.
                                        Love to all & all my love,
                                        [signature cut off at bottom of scan]
