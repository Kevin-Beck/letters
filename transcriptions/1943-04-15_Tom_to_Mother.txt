                                                  April 15, 1943
Dear Mother,
     I am afraid this will be a rather complicated letter and you may have
trouble following it. But I have a reason. Yesterday we had Mail Call for the
first time since we left the States. I received 35 to 40 letters and 4
packages. I was a little bewildered with so many letters at one time and just
looked at them trying to decide which one to open first. Finally I decided to
open your lastest letter and then the rest of yours chronologically, next came
Betty's, and the rest were picked at random. I read yours again to-night,
[crossed out: and] but not in any order, and jotted down quite a few things to
write about. It will be like reading the dictionary, changing the subject
constantly. So be prepared for a very incoherent letter.

     I think Izzy would make a fine teacher. She is so well liked by everyone
and so many people go to her with their troubles that she must give good
advice. No one can question her intelligence nor could they find any one more
level headed. I can see only one draw-

[--- sheet 1 back: 430415pg2.jpg ---]

back - I think she will find it difficult in disciplining both pupils &
herself. That is a tough job and is my only worry on board ship.

     As for George's grades you can use mine to prove that they could be
worse, but for heaven's sake not as an example. I don't believe there is any
cause for him to worry. As for the draft, tell him to take his time and think
before he makes a decision. I don't think I am being unpatriotic in saying
this - too many young men are too concious of others in uniform and perhaps we
who are in uniform do not make it any easier but we also envie at times those
that wear civies. I hope George has no illusions of a service man's life. It
is tough and abnormal. It is time spent doing a duty that has to be done and
we are trying to do as quickly as possible so we can again go back to a normal
living at home.

     After getting that off my chest I don't know just what to say about
Bill J. and Dick B. It is nice that Bill is making the best of it and is
getting along fine. He should get some where [in rank, inserted] before it is
all over. As for Dick, I think it might be that he finds it difficult to get
accustomed to the discipline

I understand that an aviation cadets life is no dream. It is much more
strenuous than what I went through - both in studies and physically. After a
week or so Dick will begin to like, perhaps not like it but he won't mind it.
The service teaches you to fight but you can't fight the service. I might as
well include the other service men now. At one time Don W.'s rating didn't mean
anything to me but now I realize that he must be good, more ways than one, to
get to where he is now. He is getting up rather high, especially in the
regular Navy. All I can say for his brother is that I don't know why he waited
so long. She had him conned for years. I haven't seen Geo. W. but we might meet
some day.

     I am not surprised at being on the honor roll at the office. They
recognized me as an employee last year when I received the Christmas gift - my
first. Perhaps the reason they knew I worked

     The next subject is one I am not too familiar with and perhaps should be
omitted. I shall just mentioned the future ones or as some one remarked "a
little inside dope". I can't make out from yours letters if Harriet's baby came
or is she still expecting. It is difficult to picture Mary Jane as a mother. I
hope I am home shortly after Betty has hers to see U. Paul's chest come out. He
will have to stretch some if he puffs up like Dad did. Of course that is the
future and I would like to hear more about the present. Some mothers do talk
too much about their children but I know one that doesn't tell me enough and I
can include a grandmother who doesn't say enough about her grandson. She writes
while sleeps and that is about all she says. The mother did mention a few
incidents but not enough. I wish I could have been in Butler watching the
cherub and the painters. It must be interesting and amusing (at times not for
Betty) to watch him grow up. I think I am missing an important part of his

     I was quite surprised to hear of Lucy McClung's marriage. I thought she
was still going with the boy from Toledo (?) [?]. I hope that Mrs. McClung and
Betty can still keep their apartment.

     You mentioned that trip we have been talking about for the last few
years. I have taught [thought] about it many times and I have a honey planned.
I won't tell you where but I think it probably would take all summer to make
it and it isn't on water. Forget the first part of the last sentence and you
can do some work on the atlas figuring out the routes we take. I am thinking of
the new Alaskan Highway the Army is building. It would be a beautiful trip and
I will still plan for it even if it turns out to be a dud.
No one can stop me from dreaming although most of them

are pipe dreams.

     My whites and shoes arrived with the mail. I don't have any use for and
never did. All I have worn on this trip is khakies, perhaps we will start
wearing blues again but I am hoping we won't.

     You mentioned you have ceased worrying about me. That's good because
there is no need to and it won't do any good any way. You just take it easy
and assert yours rights as Pres. of Beck, Inc. Go to Butler or any other place
you desired to. Why not go to Pitts. with John over the weekends. If I am
reading between the lines what I think is there he either needs a chaperon or
Fran will need a bridesmaid.

     I appreciate the folks sending food, etc. but it is really not worth
while. There is probably more candy and cookies here than in the states and no
doubt fresher.

     If you mind isn't traveling in circles by now you are a better man than I
am and I haven't finished my list yet. No doubt I will repeat quite frequently
most of this letter when I start rereading my mail. Please forgive me if I do.
                                        Love to All,
                                             Tom
